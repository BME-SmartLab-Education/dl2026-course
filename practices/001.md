---

## 1. What you are building

A document archive — a hospital's records, a company's invoices, a library's
back catalogue — gets turned into searchable text by running OCR over scans and
photographs of the paper. Some of that paper is in poor condition. It is
creased, stained, torn, or was printed so faintly that the toner barely reached
it. Run OCR on those pages and you get noise: text that looks like text, enters
the index, and is wrong. Nobody notices until somebody searches for the document
years later and it is not there.

So the first thing such a system needs is triage: **given a photograph of a
document, how much text will an OCR engine lose on it?** Answer that cheaply and
you know which images are worth the expensive pass and which need a human, a
rescan, or the original.

That is the semester project. The full rules and the grading are in the project
description; today is about the data.

One thing to understand before you start shooting, because it shapes everything
below. The answer is **not** "does this photo look bad". Two things decide it:

- **The state of the paper.** A stain across a paragraph destroys those
  characters permanently. The same stain on an empty margin costs nothing —
  there were no characters there to lose. The pages on your desk are in various
  states on purpose.
- **The state of the photograph.** Some of what you can do to a photo is
  undone before the OCR ever sees it, and some of it is not. Section 6 is built
  entirely around that distinction, and section 3 explains why.

## 2. The target

For each photograph you predict a single number between 0 and 1:

```
usability = 1 - clip( CER_photo, 0, 1 )

CER_photo = edit_distance( OCR(photo), page_text ) / length( page_text )
```

**CER** is the character error rate: the edit distance between what the OCR
engine reads and the true text of the page, divided by the length of that text.
A CER of 0 means a perfect transcription; 1 means nothing usable came out.

**Reading the number.** 1.0 means the image costs the OCR nothing. Around 0.5
means roughly half the text is gone. 0.0 means the text is gone entirely.

**Why the page's own condition counts.** You might expect us to subtract off how
hard each document is to read in the first place, so that the label measured
only your photography. We deliberately do not. A torn corner, a coffee stain or
a faint print loses text that no photograph can recover, and in an archive that
is part of the answer, not an excuse. The question is what *this image* is worth
to the OCR engine, whatever made it that way.

A consequence worth knowing: a perfectly taken photograph of a badly damaged
sheet legitimately scores low, and it should. Do not "fix" a bad page by
photographing it more carefully. Photograph it as it is.

## 3. How the label is produced

You will not annotate anything. Every photograph is labelled automatically, and
the pipeline is worth understanding, because its properties decide what makes a
photo useful and what makes it worthless.

Each page carries **four ArUco markers**, one in each corner. They do two jobs.

**They identify the page.** Each marker encodes an integer, and the four on a
page are `4 x page_id + corner`. The pipeline detects them, divides by four, and
knows which document it is looking at, and therefore which source text to
compare against. Nothing depends on you recording anything.

**They define the geometry.** The inner corners of the four markers are four
known points on a sheet of known size. That is enough to compute a perspective
transform that flattens your photo back to a true, rectangular A4 page before
anything else happens. A photo taken from the side is straightened out
automatically.

The full chain, per photograph:

1. **Detect** the four markers. If fewer than four are found, stop: this image
   cannot be used at all.
2. **Identify** the page from the marker IDs.
3. **Rectify** the sheet to a flat A4 at 200 dpi using the marker corners.
4. **Correct the illumination**, dividing out any smooth brightness gradient
   across the page.
5. **OCR** the rectified page with Tesseract, in the correct language, since the
   pages exist in Hungarian and English versions.
6. **Compare** the output with the known source text after normalisation, giving
   the CER, and clip it into [0, 1].

Three consequences follow directly, and they are what section 6 is built on.

> **A photo whose markers cannot be found produces no label at all.** It is not
> a hard training example, it is a discarded file. This is the one rule that
> never relaxes. In the rehearsal collection, 36 photographs were lost this way,
> and every single one was a framing mistake — a marker cut off by the edge of
> the frame or hidden under a thumb.

> **Angle costs you almost nothing.** Because step 3 undoes perspective, a 47
> degree shot scored the same as a straight-down one, and marker detection
> survived past 60 degrees. So vary your angle freely; it is free realism.

> **Dim light and shadow cost almost nothing either.** Step 4 removes smooth
> illumination gradients, and your phone removes most of the rest before the
> file is even saved. We measured this on the rehearsal photographs, each
> condition paired against the same student's clean shot of the same page:

| what the photographer did | cost to the label |
|---|---|
| dim light, standing still | 0.00 |
| a shadow across the page | 0.00 |
| reflection from a lamp | 0.02 |
| a shot from 47 degrees | 0.00 |
| **walking as you shoot** | **0.53** |

A clean shot scores 0.977 on average. A shot taken while walking scores 0.450.

Only the last one survives. A phone spends its entire processing budget undoing
dim light, shadow and reflection — those move information around, and moving it
back is a solved problem. What no phone can undo is information that is gone: a
stroke smeared across three characters, or ink that never reached the paper.

**This is why the shot list asks for movement and not for darkness.** It is the
only instruction we have measured that reliably damages a photograph.

## 4. What we do with it afterwards

The labelled photographs become the training set for the semester project: a
model that predicts usability from an image. You will build a classical baseline
from handcrafted image features first, then a convolutional network, and compare
them honestly.

The competition test set is photographed separately, from **fifteen document
layouts that are not in today's pool at all**, by people who are not taking part.
So a model that memorises the documents in front of you today will score well on
your own validation split and badly on the leaderboard. Which brings us to the
one methodological warning worth giving in advance.

**Do not split your data randomly by image.** The same page appears in dozens of
photographs, every layout exists in a Hungarian and an English version typeset
identically, and each student's fifty photos share a phone and a room. A random
split puts near-identical photos on both sides, and your validation score
becomes fiction. We measured it on the rehearsal data: a naive per-image split
flatters RMSE by **29 %** against an honest split that holds out whole students.
Hold out groups — by page *and* by student. The project template has a place for
this and a test that checks it.

## 6. Your assignment: 50 photographs

Use your own phone, default camera settings. Install nothing.

You are given a row from the assignment table naming **five pages**, one for each
round. You do not choose them, and you do not need to write anything down.

### The five rounds

Each round is one sheet and ten photographs of it:

1. Take the page your row names for this round. Its number is printed on the
   **back** of the sheet — that side is never photographed.
2. Shoot the ten photographs below, all of that one page.
3. Hand it on, and take the next page on your list.

Five rounds, five pages, fifty photographs. The assignment is built so that no
two people need the same sheet at the same time, so there is nothing to wait
for — but hand a sheet on as soon as you are finished with it.

### The ten photographs of a round

| how many | what to do |
|---|---|
| **3** | Braced, in room light, holding still. Sharp. |
| **4** | **Walking as you shoot.** Do not stop, do not steady yourself. |
| **3** | Out in the corridor with the lights off, or in the darkest corner of the room, **walking**, without waiting for the phone to focus. |

**Every one of the ten from a different angle.** Never two identical set-ups of
the same sheet: from above, from the side, from a corner, nearer, further. Tilt
freely — just do not shoot along the plane of the paper, and keep all four
markers in the frame.

Seven of the ten ask you to move, and that is deliberate. If you do not actually
move, no degradation is produced, and your ten photographs of that sheet are
worth about as much as one.

### Some sheets are already damaged, and some are printed faintly

Before you start: several sheets in the room are stained, creased, torn or
printed with deliberately low toner. **They are not misprints and you will not
be given a replacement.** They are the point of the exercise.

Photograph them exactly as handed to you. Do not smooth a crease, do not fold a
corner back, do not wipe a stain. If a curled sheet will not lie flat, hold it
down with a finger — a finger at the edge of the frame is realistic and
harmless, as long as it does not cover a marker.

### How degraded is degraded enough?

Aim for the **middle**, not for ruined.

> The photo should still look like a readable document at a glance, but you
> would not want to hand it in.

A barely-degraded photo scores above 0.9 nine times out of ten, and a completely
wrecked one scores near 0. Both are easy for any model and carry little
information. The photographs that decide the competition are the ones in
between, and they are the hardest to produce on purpose — which is exactly why
we are asking people rather than simulating them.

Use the first round to learn what the middle looks like on your phone. Do not
agonise over single shots; the pipeline measures the actual degradation, not
what you intended.

### Why the counts are counts and not suggestions

A model learns nothing about a kind of damage it never sees. We tested this by
training with one defect type removed: on photographs showing that defect, the
model scored **worse than guessing the average**, while remaining perfectly
accurate on everything else. A missing condition is not a small weakness spread
thinly, it is a hole.

So if the room collectively produces very few moving shots, every model built
this semester will be bad at motion, and nothing afterwards can repair it.

## 7. Rules

- **Photograph the printed side, with all four corner markers visible.** No
  exceptions. If a marker is only a smudge, that photo is a discarded file, not
  a hard example. The sheet's number is on the back; that side is never
  photographed.
- **Every shot from a different angle.** Never two identical set-ups of the same
  sheet.
- **Do not repair the sheets.** Many are creased, stained, torn or faintly
  printed on purpose. Do not flatten, smooth, fold back or clean anything. If a
  curled sheet will not lie still, hold it down with a finger.
- **No editing.** No filters, no cropping, no rotation, no "enhance", no
  document-scanner mode in the camera app, no flash. Turn HDR off if you can.
  Upload exactly what the camera saved.
- **Do not retake a photo to improve it.** A mediocre photo is data. Delete only
  genuine mistakes, such as a shot of the table.
- **Keep your phone's default resolution.** Do not downscale.

## 9. Privacy

Every document is **synthetic**. The names, addresses, company details and
figures are randomly generated and refer to no real person or organisation.
Nothing printed on these pages is personal data.

Do not photograph anything else — no faces, no other documents, no screens. If
something of yours appears in the background of a shot, delete it and retake.

We strip EXIF metadata from every image before the dataset is released,
including GPS coordinates and device information. Device details are kept only
in our own records, for checking that the collection covers a range of phones;
they are not part of the data anyone trains on.

## 10. Common mistakes

- **Not actually moving.** Seven of your ten shots per round ask for movement. A
  photo taken while standing still in a dim room is a clean photo; the phone
  will see to that.
- **Losing a marker** off the edge of the frame or under a thumb. This was the
  only cause of lost photographs in the rehearsal, and it cost 36 of them.
- **Cropping to "just the page"**, which removes the margin the detector needs.
- **Repairing a damaged sheet** before photographing it, or asking for a clean
  replacement.
- **Holding on to a sheet** after you have finished the round. Somebody else is
  waiting for it.
- **Ten near-identical photographs** of the same sheet from the same position.
- **Uploading HEIC** without checking that it converts.
